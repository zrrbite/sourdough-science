# Ester Chemistry: Where Brewing Meets Baking

The hybrid approach puts a commercial yeast and a levain's LAB in one dough, and with them two pools of flavour precursors. Esters are where that combination is most easily misread. They are the fruity end of the flavour, and brewing has worked out how they are made: mostly by enzymes, not by the textbook reaction of an acid with an alcohol.

## The Precursor Pools

Commercial yeast produces ethanol and higher (fusel) alcohols as primary metabolic byproducts: isoamyl alcohol, isobutanol, 2-phenylethanol, and others. The sourdough levain contributes lactic and acetic acid from LAB metabolism. On paper, an acid and an alcohol condense to an ester and water: ethanol + acetic acid → ethyl acetate (fruity, solvent-like), ethanol + lactic acid → ethyl lactate (creamy). In a dough that direct reaction is the minor route. Most esters are made by enzymes, and most of those enzymes do not use the levain's acids at all.

## Enzymatic vs. Chemical Esterification

**The chemical route is slow, and in water it runs the wrong way.** Without a catalyst, an acid and an alcohol react very slowly at fermentation temperatures. The equilibrium is also against it: the reaction releases water, and in a dough water is in vast excess, which favours hydrolysis of esters back to acid and alcohol. This is general chemistry; no measurement in dough was found.

**The yeast's route uses acids the cell activates itself.** The alcohol acetyltransferases encoded by *ATF1* and *ATF2* join acetyl-CoA, an intracellular metabolite, to an alcohol. That gives the acetate esters: ethyl acetate, isoamyl acetate (banana), 2-phenylethyl acetate (rose, honey) (Verstrepen et al. 2003). *EHT1* and *EEB1* join a medium-chain fatty acyl-CoA to ethanol, giving ethyl esters such as ethyl hexanoate (apple) and ethyl octanoate (Saerens et al. 2010). The acyl donor is made inside the cell, so the levain's lactic and acetic acid are not the feedstock. The alcohols are, which is why more fusel alcohol tends to mean more acetate esters (chapter 07). These are the same enzymes and the same flavours as in brewing. Whether a given yeast carries the genes varies by strain (Pradal et al. 2025).

**Some LAB make esters too.** In a medium built to resemble wheat sourdough, strains of several sourdough LAB species made ethyl acetate and ethyl lactate, and how much they made was limited by the supply of precursors. The esterase genes thought to be responsible vary by species, and the *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* strains tested made neither ester (Pradal et al. 2025). Ethyl lactate is built from lactic acid and ethanol, so this route plausibly needs both partners: the LAB's acid and the yeast's alcohol. It is the one place where the acid-plus-alcohol picture comes close to true, and it runs through an enzyme.

During a cold retard, all of these enzymes keep working at reduced rates.

## What the Oven Does

The bake decides how much of the esters survive; it is not where they are made. Esters and ethanol are volatile. The crumb stabilises near 98°C (limited by water's boiling point) while water leaves through a crust that climbs past 150°C, carrying volatiles with it.

The crust's extra complexity comes from Maillard and Strecker chemistry: pyrazines, furanones, Strecker aldehydes and melanoidins, built from the sugars and amino acids that fermentation left behind (chapter 14). In pumpernickel, the bread's aroma formed mostly during baking and was led by malty 3-methylbutanal and 2-methylbutanal and potato-like methional, while the esters were among the compounds whose levels the fermentation set (Majcher et al. 2020). Pumpernickel bakes for many hours at low heat, so it is an extreme case, but it points the same way. No source was found for esters forming in the crust.

## What the Hybrid Adds

Because esters are made by enzymes during fermentation, any dough with an active yeast makes the acetate and ethyl esters; they do not need a levain. What the hybrid adds is a large, active population of commercial *S. cerevisiae*, the species whose ester enzymes brewing has characterised, alongside a levain whose LAB may add ethyl lactate and more ethyl acetate.

A shorter ferment does not help by saving alcohols for a reaction in the oven, since the oven does not make esters. It may help more simply. Esters are volatile, and CO₂ leaving the dough during folds and degassing plausibly carries some of them away (chapter 16), so a shorter ferment may keep more of what the yeast made. That is a hypothesis; no measurement in dough was found.

The brewing parallel holds, at the point where the yeast is working. In dry-hopped brewing, the hops go in while the yeast is active, so its enzymes can transform hop compounds. In a dough, the fruity character is likewise set while the yeast is active, and the oven can only keep or lose it.
