# The Kveik Parallel: Domestication Through Selective Pressure

The observation that a pure sourdough culture leavens bread after 3 hours at 40°C+ raises a fundamental question: did the baker’s practice inadvertently domesticate the culture toward thermotolerance, in the same way that Norwegian farmhouse brewers domesticated Kveik yeast over centuries of warm fermentation? The genomic and ecological evidence suggests that this is not merely an analogy — it is the same mechanism operating on different timescales.

## What Kveik Genomics Reveal About Domestication

Kveik strains are genetically *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* — the same species as standard ale yeast. Their thermotolerance is not the product of millions of years of separate speciation; it was domesticated into them over centuries of Norwegian farmhouse practice. Brewers fermented warm (often 28–40°C+ near wood-fired stoves), harvested yeast from successful batches, dried it on wooden rings or logs, and reused it for the next brew. Every generation was a selection event: yeast that thrived at high temperature survived, reproduced, and got harvested. Yeast that didn’t was outcompeted and lost.

Preiss et al. (2018, *Frontiers in Microbiology* 9:2137) performed whole genome sequencing on Kveik strains from nine Norwegian sources and found that they form a **genetically distinct phylogenetic cluster** among domesticated beer yeasts. The genomic signatures of domestication included: increased copy number variations (CNVs) of *FLO* genes (related to flocculation), CNVs of maltose and maltotriose metabolic genes (*MAL*), and loss-of-function mutations in phenolic off-flavour genes (*PDC1* and *FDC1*). Critically, phenotypic testing showed remarkable ethanol and thermotolerance — traits directly attributable to the selective pressure of warm fermentation over hundreds of yeast generations.

Foster et al. (2022, *Frontiers in Microbiology* 13:747546) further demonstrated that Kveik strains exhibit **enhanced trehalose accumulation** compared to standard ale yeasts. Trehalose is a disaccharide that stabilises protein structure and cell membrane integrity under thermal stress. The increased capacity of Kveik strains to accumulate intracellular trehalose is a direct molecular mechanism underlying their thermotolerance, and it is a trait that was selected for through centuries of warm fermentation practice.

## The Selection Mechanism: Differential Growth Under Thermal Pressure

The “battle of the microbes” in a sourdough culture operates through **differential growth rates** under the prevailing environmental conditions. When the environment changes (temperature, pH, hydration, nutrient availability), the organisms with the highest growth rates under the new conditions come to dominate the population. This is not direct combat — it is competitive exclusion through resource competition.

At 40°C, a thermotolerant yeast that can still divide once every 3–4 hours will, over the course of a single 10-hour build cycle, massively outnumber a mesophilic yeast whose division has halted entirely. After several build cycles under these conditions, the mesophilic organism is a rounding error in the population. The baker did not actively kill it — the baker made it irrelevant by creating conditions where it could not compete.

This is precisely the mechanism documented by Van der Meulen et al. (2011, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* 77:2716–2726) in a systematic study of sourdough microbiota under different temperatures and backslopping regimes. Fermentation at 23°C with daily backslopping selected for *Leuconostoc citreum* as the dominant LAB species, while fermentation at 30°C and 37°C with the same backslopping schedule selected for *Lactobacillus fermentum*. The temperature alone was sufficient to drive a complete shift in the dominant species within 10 backslopping cycles. The yeast community was similarly affected.

Urien et al. (2014, *PLOS ONE*) demonstrated the same principle over a longer timescale: 56 daily propagation cycles of spontaneously fermented rye sourdough at two temperatures. After 56 cycles at 20°C, *Kazachstania unispora* was the sole dominant yeast species. At 30°C, the yeast population was a different combination entirely, including *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Candida krusei* (now *Pichia kudriavzevii*), and *Candida glabrata* — all species with higher thermal tolerance. The temperature drove species selection even when every other variable was controlled.

## Candidate Thermotolerant Yeast Species in Sourdough

Several yeast species commonly found in sourdough ecosystems have documented thermotolerance at or above 40°C:

**Pichia kudriavzevii** (formerly *Candida krusei*): Metabolically active at temperatures up to 45°C, with robust ethanol and acid tolerance. Isolated from sourdough in multiple studies. Meroth et al. (2003, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* 69:7453–7461) specifically documented its growth at 40°C in sourdough and its tolerance of high lactic and acetic acid concentrations. Limtong et al. (2018) isolated strains from sugarcane soil that grew at 45°C and produced ethanol efficiently at 40°C. It is normally found in fermented foods, fruits, and sourdough, and is recognised as one of the most thermotolerant yeast species documented in food fermentation contexts.

**Thermotolerant wild Saccharomyces cerevisiae:** While domesticated *S. cerevisiae* ale strains typically have an upper growth limit around 37–40°C, wild and industrial strains with growth at 40–42°C have been isolated from warm-climate fermentations. Kitichantaropas et al. (2016, *AMB Express*) characterised multiple stress-tolerant *S. cerevisiae* strains showing continuous HSP expression and trehalose accumulation at elevated temperatures.

**Wickerhamomyces anomalus:** Another common sourdough yeast with moderate thermotolerance. Documented in multiple sourdough surveys and capable of growth at 35–37°C, with some strain-level variation extending this range.

## Parallel LAB Selection

The same selective pressure that reshapes the yeast population also reshapes the LAB community. At 40°C+, the classic sourdough LAB *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* (formerly *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, growth optimum ~30–32°C, upper limit ~40°C) is outcompeted by thermophilic species. Brandt et al. (2004, *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*) showed that *L. sanfranciscensis* growth and acetate production declined at 35°C while lactate production was maintained. At 40°C+, thermophilic homofermentative species such as *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* (optimum ~42–45°C), *Lactobacillus helveticus*, and *Lactobacillus fermentum* dominate.

This LAB shift has direct flavour consequences. The heterofermentative *L. sanfranciscensis* produces both lactic and acetic acid plus CO₂ and ethanol. Thermophilic homofermentative species produce primarily lactic acid. A culture that has undergone thermal selection will have a **cleaner, less vinegary acid profile** dominated by lactic acid rather than the sharp acetic character associated with traditional sourdough. Whether this is a positive or negative change is application-dependent, but for pizza — where acetic acid can tighten the dough and interfere with stretching — a lactic-dominant profile may actually be beneficial.

## Timescale: How Fast Can Selection Occur?

The difference between Kveik domestication and sourdough thermal selection is timescale and intentionality. Norwegian farmers selected Kveik deliberately for hundreds of years. A home baker applying hot builds has been doing it for months or a few years. But microbial generations are fast. *S. cerevisiae* can double every 90 minutes under optimal conditions. Even at reduced growth rates, a sourdough culture experiences hundreds to thousands of yeast generations per year. The Van der Meulen et al. (2011) data showed complete species-level population shifts within just **10 backslopping cycles** — 10 days of daily propagation was sufficient to replace the dominant species entirely.

What a home baker is most likely seeing after months of warm practice is **species-level selection** rather than the deep genomic changes seen in Kveik (gene amplification, polyploidy, novel SNPs). Thermotolerant species that were present in the original culture at low frequency — *P. kudriavzevii*, thermotolerant wild *Saccharomyces*, heat-tolerant LAB — have been amplified by the selection pressure, while thermosensitive species have been competitively excluded. The culture’s species composition has shifted, even if the individual organisms have not evolved new genomic traits.

Over years or decades of consistent hot practice, strain-level adaptation could begin to occur on top of species-level selection — the same process that produced Kveik, operating on a shorter but still meaningful timescale. The culture in the jar is not static; it is evolving under the selective pressure of the baker’s practice.

## The Kitchen as Selective Environment

Direct backslopping from hot builds is not the only selection pathway. The kitchen itself is an ecosystem. Flour dust, surfaces, the air, the baker’s hands — these carry microbes from baking activities back to the starter jar at every feed. If the baker’s practice consistently involves high temperatures, the environmental microbial pool in the kitchen shifts toward thermotolerant organisms, and these continuously inoculate the mother culture. The selection pressure is ambient and ongoing, even if the mother culture is nominally maintained at room temperature.

This is consistent with the findings of De Vuyst et al. (2018, *Frontiers in Microbiology*), who identified house microbiota — the microbial consortium contaminating bakery surfaces and equipment — as a significant source of inoculation for sourdough cultures. Metabolically active *L. sanfranciscensis* was found on storage boxes and dough mixers, demonstrating that dominant sourdough organisms colonise the production environment and re-inoculate the culture from there. A kitchen where hot fermentation is practised would harbour a different environmental microbiome than one where cool fermentation is the norm.

## The Baker as Yeast Domesticator

The implication is that every sourdough baker is, whether they know it or not, a yeast domesticator. The feeding schedule, the fermentation temperature, the hydration, the flour choice, and the kitchen environment all constitute selection pressures that shape the culture’s microbial composition over time. A baker who consistently ferments warm is selecting for thermotolerance. A baker who uses whole grain feeds is selecting for organisms that tolerate higher mineral content and enzymatic activity. A baker who ferments stiff is selecting for acetic-acid-tolerant organisms.

The culture in the jar is a product of its selective history, and its performance reflects the conditions under which it has been maintained. A portion of a heat-adapted culture shared with another baker may perform well at 40°C for them too, because the organisms are already selected. A portion of a cool-maintained culture given in return would likely fail at the same hot protocol. The culture carries its selective history with it.

This understanding opens the possibility of **intentional selection**: deliberately building hot over many cycles to push the culture toward thermotolerance, while maintaining a room-temperature backup for safety. The sourdough culture becomes a tool that the baker shapes through selective pressure, just as brewers shaped Kveik over centuries. The timescale is faster (microbial generations are measured in hours, not human lifetimes), the mechanism is identical, and the outcome is a culture with baking-relevant phenotypes that most sourdough cultures do not possess.
