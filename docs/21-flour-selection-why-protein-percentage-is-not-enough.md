# Flour Selection: Why Protein Percentage Is Not Enough

The choice of flour is arguably the single most consequential variable in a long-fermented pizza dough, yet it is routinely reduced to a single number: protein percentage. This is inadequate. Two flours at identical protein content can produce fundamentally different doughs because protein percentage tells you nothing about protein **quality**, gluten **strength**, or the **balance** between extensibility and elasticity.

## Protein Quality: HMW Glutenin Subunit Composition

The protein percentage on a flour bag is derived from Kjeldahl nitrogen analysis — it measures total nitrogen content and converts to a protein estimate by a multiplication factor. It captures the total amount of protein but nothing about its **composition**. For baking performance, what matters is the glutenin-to-gliadin ratio and, more specifically, which **high-molecular-weight glutenin subunit (HMW-GS) alleles** are present in the wheat variety.

Wheat varieties carry different alleles at three genetic loci: Glu-A1, Glu-B1, and Glu-D1. These alleles produce glutenin subunits with dramatically different polymerisation behaviour. The **5+10 allele pair at Glu-D1** produces glutenin that forms longer, more extensively cross-linked polymers via disulfide bonding — tighter mesh, more elasticity, better gas retention. The **2+12 allele pair** at the same locus produces shorter, weaker polymers. Both contribute equally to total protein percentage on the bag, but they produce fundamentally different gluten networks and fundamentally different dough behaviour.

Premium flour producers like Caputo source wheat varieties specifically selected for favourable HMW-GS profiles. A generic flour labelled “Manitoba, 13%” may have the protein content but the wrong subunit composition — the glutenin polymerises into shorter chains, the network is weaker, and the dough underperforms despite the number on the bag looking acceptable.

## The Alveograph W Index: The Number That Actually Matters

The **alveograph W index** measures the total energy required to inflate a thin sheet of dough to bursting, expressed in joules × 10⁻⁴. It captures both the strength and extensibility of the gluten network in a single measurement, and it correlates with baking performance far better than protein percentage does.

Caputo Oro Manitoba typically sits at **W 350–380+**. A generic “Manitoba” flour at 13% protein is often **W 280–310**. That gap of 50–80 W units is enormous in practice. It means the Caputo network absorbs significantly more energy before failing — it stretches further, holds more gas pressure, and survives longer fermentation without structural collapse. For a 48h cold retard, this difference determines whether the dough arrives at the oven with its gluten network intact or partially degraded beyond recovery.

## The P/L Ratio: Strength vs. Extensibility Balance

The alveograph also reports **P** (resistance to deformation, or tenacity) and **L** (extensibility before rupture). The ratio P/L describes the balance between these two properties. For pizza and long-fermented doughs, the ideal P/L is approximately **0.5–0.7** — extensible enough to stretch thin without tearing, strong enough to hold gas during proof and oven spring.

Caputo Oro is formulated to hit this balance. A generic flour might have high P and low L (strong but tears during stretching — the dough snaps back aggressively and rips when you try to open it) or low P and high L (extensible but collapses under its own weight during proofing — the dough puddles on the peel). The protein percentage tells you nothing about which of these failure modes you are headed toward. Only the P/L ratio does.

## The “Manitoba” Label: Marketing vs. Origin

Historically, **Manitoba** refers to hard red spring wheat from the Canadian prairies — varieties classified as CWRS (Canada Western Red Spring) that have been bred for over a century for specific gluten characteristics: high protein content, favourable HMW-GS allele profiles, high W index, balanced P/L. These varietal characteristics are the product of deliberate breeding programmes optimising for baking performance.

In the European retail market, “Manitoba” has become an **essentially unregulated marketing term** meaning roughly “high protein.” There is no requirement that the wheat originates from Manitoba, or that it shares the varietal characteristics of genuine CWRS wheat. A bag labelled “Manitoba, 13%” could contain Eastern European wheat, a blend from multiple origins, or any variety that happens to hit the protein number. The label tells you the nitrogen content; it guarantees nothing about the genetics, the gluten quality, or the baking performance.

Caputo actually sources specific wheat varieties and controls the varietal composition of their blends. When they put “Oro Manitoba” on the bag, the Manitoba designation refers to the type of wheat used, not just a protein target.

## Milling: Particle Size and Damaged Starch

Beyond wheat variety, the milling process itself affects dough performance through two mechanisms: **particle size distribution** and **damaged starch**.

Damaged starch granules are created when the milling rollers physically fracture starch granules during grinding. These fractured granules are the primary substrate for amylase enzymes — more damage means more starch surface area exposed to enzymatic hydrolysis, which means more sugar liberation during fermentation. For a 48h cold retard, lower damaged starch is preferable: it slows the rate of sugar liberation, giving the yeast more time to consume available sugars before they accumulate. This directly affects the sugar depletion dynamics discussed in the high-heat baking section — a flour with high damaged starch will have more residual sugar at the end of the same retard period, increasing burn risk in an Ooni.

Caputo controls damaged starch levels through calibrated milling parameters. A generic mill optimising for throughput and cost may produce more damaged starch as a byproduct of aggressive roller settings. The difference is invisible on the label but measurable in the oven.

## Ash Content and Enzymatic Activity

As discussed earlier, ash content is a proxy for mineral content — higher ash means more bran and aleurone layer material in the flour, which carries mineral cofactors (calcium, magnesium, zinc, phosphorus) that activate endogenous enzymes. A higher-ash flour has more active amylases and proteases, which accelerates both sugar liberation and gluten degradation during long fermentation.

Caputo Oro sits at approximately **0.45–0.55% ash** (Tipo 00 specification). This low ash content limits enzymatic activity during long retards, preserving the gluten network and moderating sugar production. A generic flour with 0.65–0.80% ash (even at the same protein content) will have noticeably more enzymatic activity — faster gluten degradation, more sugar liberation, narrower timing window, and greater burn risk at high temperatures.

## Practical Flour Selection for the Hybrid Formula

For the hybrid pizza dough with a 48h cold retard:

**First choice:** Caputo Oro Manitoba or equivalent premium Tipo 00 with W 320+, P/L 0.5–0.7, protein 13.5–14.5%, ash <0.55%. This flour is engineered for long-fermented applications and will survive a 48h retard with its network intact.

**Acceptable alternative:** Any Tipo 00 or strong bread flour with verified W 300+. If the W index is not available on the label or from the manufacturer, protein percentage above 13.5% from a reputable mill is a reasonable (though imperfect) proxy.

**Use with caution:** Generic “Manitoba” flour under 13% protein or with unknown W index. These flours may perform well in short-fermented or direct doughs but are likely to show structural degradation during a 48h cold retard. If using them, consider shortening the retard to 24h, reducing the levain percentage (less acid = less protease activation), or blending with 20–30% Caputo Oro to boost gluten quality.

**Levain flour:** The flour used to feed the levain can differ from the main dough flour. A whole wheat or high-extraction flour for the levain provides mineral cofactors, additional flavour precursors, and enzymatic complexity — but confined to the 10–15% of total flour that the levain represents. This gives flavour benefits from a complex flour without exposing the bulk dough to its higher enzymatic activity.

The core message: for a 2-hour direct dough, flour differences are marginal. For a 48h cold-retarded hybrid dough in an Ooni, the flour is the difference between a dough that arrives at the oven structurally intact and one that doesn’t. Invest in the flour; it is the single highest-impact ingredient choice.
