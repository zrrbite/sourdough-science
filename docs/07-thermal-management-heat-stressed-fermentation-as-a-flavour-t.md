# Thermal Management: Heat-Stressed Fermentation as a Flavour Tool

Conventional baking advice is thermally cautious: keep levain builds at 25–28°C, bulk ferment around 22–25°C, and avoid pushing temperatures higher. This advice is calibrated for schedule predictability and consistent leavening, not for optimal flavour compound production. Applying intense heat to levains and dough — deliberately pushing yeast toward its thermal stress threshold — produces a fundamentally different and often superior metabolite profile.

## The Yeast Heat Shock Response

When *S. cerevisiae* or wild sourdough yeasts approach their thermal stress threshold (roughly 35–40°C for most strains), they do not simply suffer passively. They activate a coordinated **heat shock response**: upregulation of heat shock proteins (HSPs) that act as molecular chaperones, accumulation of trehalose as an intracellular thermoprotectant, and changes in cell membrane composition (a shift toward more saturated fatty acids for thermal stability). The organism actively remodels itself to survive.

The metabolic consequences of this stress response are where the flavour benefits emerge. Heat-stressed yeast shifts its metabolic profile in ways that produce **more flavour compounds, not fewer**.

## Increased Fusel Alcohol and Ester Production

At elevated temperatures, **fusel alcohol production increases significantly**. Isoamyl alcohol, isobutanol, and 2-phenylethanol all accumulate at higher rates because the biosynthetic pathways (the Ehrlich pathway, amino acid catabolism) have higher throughput at elevated temperatures. These higher alcohols are the precursors for the complex esters that contribute fruity, floral, and aromatic notes to the finished product.

Simultaneously, the ATF1 and ATF2 alcohol acetyltransferase enzymes responsible for enzymatic ester synthesis show elevated activity at higher temperatures (Verstrepen et al., 2003; Saerens et al., 2010). Industrial-scale brewing data support the net effect: Kucharczyk and Tuszyński (2018) found that both esters and higher alcohols increased significantly as fermentation temperature rose, and Landaud et al. (2001) showed temperature driving fusel alcohol production independently of pressure.

**A caveat on conflicting evidence.** The relationship between temperature and ester yield is not uniformly positive across the literature. Ferrández-Gómez et al. (2024) tested sourdough-derived yeast strains in beer wort and found that fermentation at 37°C produced a *drastic reduction* in volatile organic compounds, esters especially — the opposite of the brewing-temperature trend reported by Landaud and Kucharczyk. The discrepancy is most plausibly explained by strain identity and matrix effects: enzymatic synthesis rate and volatile loss rate both rise with temperature, and which dominates depends on the strain’s thermal optimum, the fermentation vessel’s headspace and surface-to-volume ratio, and the duration of heat exposure. Brief hot phases in a closed or dough-bound matrix likely favour net accumulation; extended hot fermentation in an open or low-viscosity matrix likely favours net loss. The practical implication is that a baker cannot assume higher temperature means more esters — it must be verified by taste against a room-temperature control.

This thermal flavour enhancement is directly analogous to Kveik fermentation in brewing, where Norwegian farmhouse strains produce clean, ester-forward beer at 35–43°C. The parallel runs deeper than flavour — it extends to how a culture becomes thermotolerant in the first place — and is developed in full in the dedicated Kveik section later in this document.

## LAB Thermotolerance and Population Dynamics

The bacterial side of the equation is equally important. Most LAB species in a sourdough culture are significantly more thermotolerant than the wild yeast. Some are outright thermophilic: *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* has a growth optimum around 42–45°C, and many other sourdough-associated *Lactobacillus* species remain fully active at 35–40°C.

At temperatures where yeast is stressed but functional, LAB are **thriving**. The population balance shifts toward more bacterial dominance: more acid production (both lactic and acetic), more proteolytic activity from LAB proteases, and more LAB-derived flavour metabolites (diacetyl, acetaldehyde, organic acids). This is a controlled shift in population dynamics — giving the LAB an advantage without killing the yeast.

The practical result is a levain that is more **flavour-dense per unit of time**. What would normally take 8–12 hours at 25°C is compressed into a shorter, more intense fermentation window. The peak organism count is similar, but the metabolite profile is richer — more esters, more fusel alcohols, more acid-derived flavour compounds per gram of levain.

## The Hot Sprint, Cold Coast Strategy

The most effective way to exploit heat-stressed fermentation in the hybrid pizza context is a two-phase thermal strategy: a **hot sprint** followed by a **cold coast**.

During the **hot sprint** (the levain build and/or the initial mix and brief bulk ferment at 35–40°C), yeast and LAB are under thermal stress and producing flavour metabolites at elevated rates. Fusel alcohols accumulate, ester synthesis accelerates, and the LAB contribute a richer organic acid profile than a room-temperature ferment would produce. This phase generates a dense pool of flavour precursors in a short window.

During the **cold coast** (the 48–72h cold retard at 3–5°C), metabolic activity drops dramatically. The flavour precursors generated during the hot sprint are **preserved** rather than consumed or off-gassed, because the low temperature suppresses further metabolism. Meanwhile, the time-dependent gluten development mechanisms (diffusion bonding, oxidative cross-linking, internal gas pressure) continue building the network, Maillard precursors accumulate from slow enzymatic starch and protein breakdown, and sugars are depleted for high-heat baking.

The result is a dough that enters the oven with a richer flavour precursor base than either a purely cold process or a purely warm process could produce. The hot sprint generates what the cold coast alone cannot (stress-induced metabolites). The cold coast preserves what the hot sprint would otherwise lose (volatile precursors stripped by continued active fermentation).

## The Thermal Risk Gradient

The danger zone for yeast is not a cliff but a **gradient**. Understanding where your process sits on this gradient is essential for pushing temperature without crossing into failure.

**25–30°C:** Conventional comfort zone. Yeast and LAB both active, moderate metabolite production. No stress response. This is where most baking recipes operate.

**30–35°C:** Accelerated fermentation. Yeast is near its growth optimum, gas production and metabolite rates increase. LAB accelerate proportionally. Mild stress response begins at the upper end. Most strains handle this range comfortably.

**35–40°C:** Heat stress zone. The productive sweet spot for flavour enhancement. Yeast activates the full heat shock response: HSP upregulation, trehalose accumulation, membrane remodelling. Fusel alcohol and ester production increase significantly. LAB are thriving and may dominate the population balance. The yeast population remains viable but is under genuine stress — growth rate slows even as metabolite production increases.

**40–45°C:** Viability decline. Most wild sourdough yeast strains begin losing viability at the upper end of this range. The population declines gradually, not catastrophically. LAB (particularly thermophilic species) may still be fully active. If leavening becomes sluggish or absent after a hot build at these temperatures, the yeast population has been reduced below functional levels. The dough will still have flavour (LAB are working) but may lack gas production.

**45°C+:** Thermal kill territory for most yeast. LAB activity also declines for mesophilic species, though true thermophiles may persist. This range is not useful for dough fermentation.

Important caveat: these limits are **strain- and culture-dependent, not universal**. They describe the most commonly studied sourdough species under laboratory conditions. A culture maintained under warm conditions, or subjected to repeated thermal selection, may contain strains that exceed these limits substantially. Bakers should test their own culture empirically — if a pure sourdough levain built at 40°C+ produces a well-risen loaf, the culture is thermotolerant enough regardless of what the literature predicts for the species it ‘should’ contain. The bread is the proof.

## The Over-Proteolysis Risk at High Temperature

The other significant risk of heat-stressed fermentation is **accelerated proteolytic degradation**. More LAB activity at higher temperatures means more protease output, which means faster gluten network cleavage. For a dough going into a long cold retard, a hot front-end loads more proteolytic damage onto the network before it even hits the fridge. The network then has to survive 48–72 hours of cold retard with this head start on degradation.

This is where flour quality becomes a hard constraint rather than a preference. A high-W flour (Caputo Oro at W 350–380+) can absorb the proteolytic front-loading because the glutenin polymer chains are longer and more extensively cross-linked — there is more network to degrade before structural failure. A weaker flour (W 280–310) may not survive the combination of a hot sprint and a long cold retard; the network arrives at the oven already compromised.

Mitigation strategies include: keeping the hot sprint **brief** (the levain build can run hot for its full maturation, but the dough itself should spend no more than 30–60 minutes at elevated temperature before entering the fridge); using a **lower levain percentage** (10% rather than 15–20%, reducing the total protease load introduced into the dough); and selecting a **high-W flour** that can absorb the additional proteolytic stress.

## Practical Application: Hot Levain Build for the Hybrid Formula

The simplest way to incorporate heat-stressed fermentation into the hybrid pizza workflow is to run the **levain build hot** while keeping the dough itself at moderate or room temperature before the cold retard. This concentrates the thermal flavour enhancement in the levain (where it is most effective per gram of flour) without exposing the bulk dough to excessive proteolytic stress.

Build the levain at **35–38°C** using a proofing box, oven with light on, or warm water bath. The levain will reach peak faster (4–6 hours rather than 8–12), and the metabolite profile at peak will be richer in esters, fusel alcohols, and organic acids than a room-temperature build. Catch it just at peak — the higher activity rate also means it passes peak and enters decline faster, so the timing window is narrower.

Incorporate this hot-built levain into the dough at room temperature (~22°C). Perform the single coil fold, divide into balls, and move to the cold retard as normal. The flavour precursors from the hot levain build are now distributed throughout the dough and will be preserved during the cold coast. The bulk dough flour has not been exposed to the elevated protease activity of the hot build, so the gluten network develops normally during the retard.

For a more aggressive approach, the entire dough can be held at 30–35°C for the brief bulk period (30–60 minutes before balling and cold retarding). This pushes all the organisms — both the levain’s LAB and wild yeast, and the commercial yeast — into accelerated metabolite production. The risk of over-proteolysis increases, so this approach is best reserved for high-W flours and shorter cold retards (48h rather than 72h).
