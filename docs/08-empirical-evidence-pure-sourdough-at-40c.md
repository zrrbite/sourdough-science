# Empirical Evidence: Pure Sourdough at 40°C+

The thermal risk gradient described above is derived from published literature on the most commonly studied sourdough yeast species, particularly *Kazachstania humilis* and mesophilic wild *Saccharomyces* strains. However, empirical experimentation with pure sourdough cultures (no commercial yeast supplementation) at 40°C+ has produced results that contradict the expected outcome: the bread rose well, with full leavening from wild yeast alone. This demands a revision of the thermal assumptions.

## The Empirical Protocol

The following protocol has been tested with repeated success using a pure sourdough culture with no commercial yeast addition:

**1.** Levain build at 40°C+ for approximately 1 hour.

**2.** Levain incorporated into dough. Bulk fermentation at 40°C+ for approximately 2 hours.

**3.** Cold retard overnight (10–12 hours at 3–5°C).

**4.** Bake. Result: good rise, well-leavened crumb, no structural failure.

This result is significant because all gas production came from the wild yeast population in the sourdough culture. At temperatures where the literature predicts yeast growth inhibition or death, the culture produced sufficient CO₂ for full leavening. The bread rose. That is the data point that overrides the textbook.

## Two Hypotheses: Thermotolerance vs. Temporal Escape

There are two possible explanations for why the yeast survive and function at 40°C+, and the distinction has practical consequences.

### Hypothesis 1: Genuine Thermotolerance

The sourdough culture contains wild yeast strains that are genuinely thermotolerant — not merely surviving at 40°C but metabolically active, producing CO₂ at rates sufficient for leavening. This would mean the culture does not contain the typical *K. humilis* that Brandt et al. (2004) showed was inhibited at 35°C, or it contains a thermotolerant strain variant of it.

Several wild yeast species documented in sourdough contexts are known to grow at 40°C+. *Pichia kudriavzevii* (formerly *Candida krusei*) was shown by Meroth et al. (2003) to survive at 40°C in sourdough and to tolerate high concentrations of both lactic and acetic acid. Some wild *Saccharomyces* strains isolated from warm-climate fermentations show growth at 40–42°C. *Wickerhamomyces anomalus*, another common sourdough yeast, has moderate thermotolerance.

If a sourdough culture has been maintained in a warm kitchen environment, or has been subjected to repeated warm builds, it may have undergone a natural selection process analogous to the domestication of Kveik in Norwegian farmhouse brewing. Each warm build applies selection pressure that favours thermotolerant organisms, and over many generations the population composition shifts toward heat-adapted species and strains. The culture may be evolving into something genuinely unusual — a heat-adapted sourdough consortium distinct from the textbook *K. humilis / L. sanfranciscensis* pair.

### Hypothesis 2: Temporal Escape

The wild yeast population is mesophilic and is genuinely stressed at 40°C, but the total heat exposure (approximately 3 hours) is brief enough that a sufficient fraction of the population survives without reproducing. Thermal kill is **time-dependent**, not instantaneous. At 40°C, mesophilic yeast stop dividing and begin losing viability gradually, not catastrophically. One hour in the levain and two hours in the bulk may reduce the population significantly but not to zero.

The surviving yeast do not need to reproduce to leaven the bread. The population that existed at the moment of the levain build carries a full complement of metabolic enzymes. Even non-dividing, heat-stressed cells can produce CO₂ from sugar via glycolysis and fermentation — they are metabolically active even as they lose reproductive viability. The gas production during the 3-hour hot phase, combined with dissolved CO₂ accumulation during the overnight cold retard, may provide sufficient total leavening for a well-risen loaf.

Under this hypothesis, there is a duration threshold beyond which leavening collapses. If the hot exposure were extended to 6, 8, or 12 hours at 40°C+, the yeast population would decline below functional density and the bread would fail to rise. The brevity of the protocol is what makes it work.

## Distinguishing the Hypotheses

The practical test is straightforward: **extend the hot bulk and observe when leavening fails**. If the yeast are genuinely thermotolerant (Hypothesis 1), the dough should tolerate 4, 6, or even 8 hours at 40°C and still produce gas (though gluten structural failure may occur before yeast failure). If the yeast are surviving on borrowed time (Hypothesis 2), there is a sharp threshold — probably somewhere between 4 and 8 hours — where the population drops below functional density and leavening ceases abruptly.

A complementary test is to build the levain at 40°C+ and observe whether it **doubles in volume** during the build, or merely ferments without visible expansion. If it produces acid and flavour but does not expand significantly, the LAB are active but the yeast are not contributing gas, which points toward temporal escape — the leavening in the final bread would then come from pre-existing cells metabolising without reproducing.

A caution on reading the positive result, which an earlier version of this chapter stated too strongly. **Doubling demonstrates gas production, not cell division.** Heat-stressed, non-dividing yeast are still metabolically active and still evolve CO₂, which is exactly what Hypothesis 2 predicts. A negative result is therefore informative and a positive result is not decisive. The test that does separate the hypotheses is **serial hot passage**: at a 1:1:1 feed the incoming starter is a third of the new mass, so a non-dividing population falls to (⅓)ⁿ of its density after n builds — under a two-hundredth by the fifth. If a levain backslopped from the hot jar five builds running still rises like the first, the population is replenishing itself, and nothing but division explains that. The bench protocol for both is in [The Levain Doubling Test](../notes/levain-doubling-test.md).

A note elsewhere in this repository takes a position on these two hypotheses rather than leaving them balanced: [Why the Hot Protocol Works](../notes/why-the-hot-protocol-works.md) argues that temporal escape, compounded with thermal lag, the protective ramp, the fresh-feed-before-heat pattern, low water activity and the cool-core refuge, is sufficient to explain the result without an unusual culture. It is a bet rather than a finding, and it names the levain-doubling test above as the one that would falsify it.

## Implications for Culture Maintenance

Under either hypothesis, the question of how the mother culture is maintained between bakes is critical.

If the mother culture is **fed and maintained at room temperature** (20–25°C), and only the production levain is built hot at 40°C+, then the mother culture never experiences the thermal selection pressure. The hot build is a terminal event: the levain goes into the dough, the dough gets baked, and the culture lineage continues at room temperature. Under this workflow, there is no long-term diversity risk. The mother culture maintains its full yeast-LAB consortium, and each hot production build draws from this intact reservoir.

If the baker is **backslopping from the hot build** — taking a portion of the 40°C+ levain to seed the next build, and repeating this cycle — then serial passage under thermal selection pressure will progressively reshape the culture. Thermotolerant organisms (both yeast and LAB) will dominate; mesophilic organisms will decline over 10–20 cycles. The culture adapts to the conditions it repeatedly experiences. This may be desirable if the goal is to develop a heat-adapted culture, but it reduces ecosystem diversity and resilience.

The practical implication is that bakers should **test their own culture’s thermal performance empirically** rather than relying solely on published species-level data. If a pure sourdough levain built at 40°C+ produces a well-risen loaf, the culture is thermotolerant enough for that protocol regardless of what the literature says about the species it ‘should’ contain. The bread is the proof. Published data provides a useful starting framework, but the culture in the jar is the final authority.
